2.1 Background and Context
The global educational landscape is undergoing a profound paradigm shift, driven by the convergence of pedagogical innovation and digital transformation. Often conceptualized as "Education 4.0," this movement seeks to leverage emerging technologies—such as Artificial Intelligence (AI), the Internet of Things (IoT), and Big Data analytics—to create personalized, efficient, and data-driven learning environments. Within this broader context of modernization, the administrative infrastructure of higher education institutions faces critical scrutiny. As universities modernize their curricula to prepare students for a digital economy, their operational backbones often remain tethered to legacy processes that are labor-intensive, prone to human error, and vulnerable to manipulation. Among these persistent administrative bottlenecks, the tracking and verification of student attendance stands out as a fundamental yet consistently inefficient operational task.
In the specific context of higher education, attendance is not merely a bureaucratic formality or a relic of disciplinary control; it is a critical pedagogical metric. Attendance data serves as a primary indicator of student engagement, a predictor of academic performance, and a mechanism for identifying at-risk individuals who may require early intervention. Furthermore, for many courses—particularly those with practical laboratory components, clinical rotations, or continuous assessment models—physical presence is a non-negotiable prerequisite for grading and accreditation. Consequently, the integrity and accuracy of attendance records are of paramount importance.
However, the prevailing methods for recording this essential data have largely failed to evolve alongside the technological capabilities of the 21st century. In many institutions, including leading universities in Vietnam such as Vietnam National University, Hanoi (VNU) and Ho Chi Minh City University of Technology (HCMUT), the standard approach involves manual verification methods that are operationally inefficient. The most common method, the verbal roll-call, requires the lecturer to read names from a roster while students respond verbally. In a typical lecture hall containing 50 to 100 students, this process can consume between 10 to 15 minutes of valuable instructional time. This represents a staggering 10-15% of a standard 90-minute lecture slot lost to administrative overhead. Over the course of a 15-week semester, this accumulates to hours of lost teaching time, directly impacting the quality of education delivery. Alternatively, the circulation of paper sign-in sheets offers a less disruptive method during the lecture but shifts the burden to the post-class phase. This method creates a significant administrative load, as the data must be manually transcribed from paper into a digital student information system. This data entry phase introduces a high risk of human error, where "present" marks can be inadvertently missed or assigned to the wrong student ID due to illegible handwriting or clerical mistakes.
Beyond the issues of operational inefficiency and data entry errors, the most critical flaw in traditional attendance systems is the lack of robust identity verification. Both verbal responses and paper signatures rely heavily on "honor systems" that are easily circumvented by students. A pervasive issue in universities globally is "proxy attendance," colloquially known as "buddy punching," where a student marks an absent peer as present. This practice undermines the academic integrity of the institution and falsifies the data used for student assessment.
Technological attempts to mitigate these issues have seen mixed success. Solutions such as Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) card tapping or Quick Response (QR) code scanning have been implemented in various institutions to automate the data collection process. However, these technologies fundamentally solve the wrong problem: they verify token possession rather than identity. An RFID system verifies that a valid student card was tapped against a reader, but it cannot verify who is holding the card. It is a common practice for students to carry multiple cards to tap in for absent friends. Similarly, dynamic QR codes displayed on a projector verify that a valid code was scanned, but these codes can be photographed and shared instantly via messaging applications, allowing absent students to check in remotely from their dormitories or homes.
Therefore, there is a pressing and unaddressed need for a solution that utilizes Biometrics—specifically Facial Recognition—to inextricably link the check-in event to the physical presence of the specific individual. Unlike tokens, passwords, or codes, biometric traits cannot be lost, forgotten, or easily shared, making them the only viable solution for ensuring true presence.
The urgency for such automation is particularly acute in the rapidly developing higher education sector of Vietnam. Institutions like FPT University, known for their focus on technology, are managing rapidly growing student populations. With thousands of students enrolled in hundreds of concurrent sessions, the administrative overhead of manual tracking scales linearly, creating an unsustainable burden on academic staff. Furthermore, the Vietnamese Ministry of Education and Training (MOET) has actively encouraged the application of AI and digital technologies in school management as part of a broader national digital transformation strategy. While some institutions have experimented with digital solutions, anecdotal evidence and student feedback suggest that even existing digital solutions often lack the speed or anti-spoofing reliability required for seamless operation. A system that is slow, causing queues at the lecture hall door, or easily spoofed, undermining academic integrity, will ultimately fail to achieve adoption.
This project proposes an AI-Driven Facial Recognition Attendance System designed specifically to address these dual challenges of Operational Efficiency and Academic Integrity within the Vietnamese educational context. By leveraging state-of-the-art Deep Learning models for facial recognition and advanced computer vision techniques for liveness detection, this system aims to provide a frictionless, secure, and automated alternative to the obsolete manual methods currently in use.
2.2 Problem Statement
The central research problem addressed by this project is the lack of a secure, automated, and scalable attendance verification system in educational environments that can reliably distinguish between genuine student presence and fraudulent attempts. The current landscape of attendance solutions presents a technological "Trilemma," where existing systems can typically achieve only two of the three necessary qualities: Convenience, Security, and Automation.
A system that is convenient and automated, such as a QR code scanner or a mobile app check-in, is rarely secure; it is susceptible to token sharing and remote check-ins. Conversely, a system that is secure and automated, such as a fingerprint scanner or iris recognition, is rarely convenient; it requires physical contact with a sensor or close proximity, creating hygiene concerns and bottlenecks at the classroom entrance. Manual systems, while potentially secure if the lecturer rigorously checks IDs, fail entirely on the metric of automation and convenience.
Facial recognition offers a unique potential to solve this trilemma by providing a non-intrusive, contactless, and automated method of verification. However, standard implementations of facial recognition suffer from a critical vulnerability: Presentation Attacks. A naive facial recognition system, which simply compares a 2D image from a camera to a database of enrolled faces, can be easily circumvented by holding up a high-resolution photograph or a video of an enrolled student. This vulnerability renders standard facial recognition insecure for academic grading purposes, as it cannot distinguish between a live human being and an inanimate representation.
Therefore, the specific technical problem to be solved is not merely the recognition of faces, but "ensuring liveness in an automated, real-time facial recognition pipeline." The challenge lies in engineering a system that can perform complex biometric analysis—verifying both identity and liveness—within a timeframe that does not impede the flow of students entering a classroom, all while running on commodity hardware to ensure cost-effectiveness for educational institutions.
2.3 Project Aim and Objectives
The primary aim of this project is to engineer an end-to-end, automated attendance system that leverages deep learning to identify students in real-time while neutralizing identity fraud through advanced anti-spoofing mechanisms. The system is designed to be deployed as a robust client-server architecture, separating the edge processing of video data from the central management of academic records, thereby minimizing hardware costs while maximizing system scalability.
To achieve this overarching aim, the project is structured around four specific, measurable objectives:
2.3.1 Objective 1: Develop a Robust Computer Vision Pipeline
The first objective is to create the "eyes" of the system—a computer vision module capable of reliable face detection and recognition. This involves implementing a video acquisition module using the OpenCV library that can interface seamlessly with standard USB webcams. The core of this pipeline integrates state-of-the-art Deep Learning models, specifically utilizing the DeepFace framework to wrap advanced architectures like FaceNet or VGG-Face. These models are tasked with generating 128-dimensional face embeddings—vector representations of facial features that are invariant to minor changes in lighting, pose, and expression. The objective is to achieve a recognition accuracy, measured by the F1-Score, of greater than 90% under the variable lighting conditions typical of a university classroom environment. Without a high-accuracy recognition baseline, the system cannot function as a valid replacement for manual checks, as frequent false negatives would frustrate users and necessitate manual intervention.
2.3.2 Objective 2: Engineer Advanced Anti-Spoofing Mechanisms
This is the critical security objective that distinguishes this project from a simple computer vision exercise. The system must not merely answer the question "Who is this?" but must also rigorously answer "Is this a live person?". This objective involves researching and implementing Liveness Detection techniques to counter presentation attacks. The solution utilizes Google Mediapipe to extract a dense 3D Face Mesh consisting of 468 unique landmarks. By analyzing the geometry of this mesh, the system can estimate depth and surface structure, distinguishing the complex topography of a human face from the flat plane of a photograph. Furthermore, the system implements heuristic checks such as Blink Detection, calculating the Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR) to detect natural biological motion that static photos lack. This objective directly addresses the failure of simple 2D recognition systems and ensures the system is robust against proxy attendance.
2.3.3 Objective 3: Implement a Scalable Client-Server Architecture
The system utilizes a hybrid processing model. To minimize server load from invalid requests, the Kiosk functions as a security gatekeeper, performing real-time Liveness Detection at the edge. Only when a subject is confirmed 'Live' does the Kiosk transmit the captured face image (Base64 encoded) to the server.

The Server then assumes responsibility for the computationally intensive tasks of Face Encoding (CNN inference) and Vector Matching, ensuring the heavy deep learning models run on centralized hardware.

2.3.4 Objective 4: Develop a Comprehensive Management Dashboard
The final objective is to make the collected data actionable for academic staff. Raw database records are of little use to a lecturer in the middle of a class. This objective involves building a Single Page Application (SPA) Web Dashboard using modern JavaScript technologies. The dashboard serves as the administrative control center, allowing lecturers to create, start, and stop attendance sessions. Crucially, it implements real-time data visualization using libraries like Chart.js to show attendance trends, status distributions, and live check-in feeds. This objective bridges the gap between the AI backend and the human decision-makers, providing a user-friendly interface for enrollment, session management, and data export in standard formats like CSV for integration with other university systems.

The scope of this project is rigorously defined to ensure the feasibility of completion within the constraints of the final year academic schedule while delivering a fully functional prototype that meets the core objectives of automated attendance and anti-spoofing security. The primary focus is centered on the application of Facial Recognition as the sole biometric modality for identity verification. While other biometric indicators such as gait analysis, voice recognition, or iris scanning offer potential avenues for identification, they fall outside the purview of this research due to the specific hardware requirements and the complexities involved in multi-modal fusion.
The operational environment for the system is calibrated specifically for indoor classroom settings characterized by controlled artificial lighting. This constraint acknowledges the variability inherent in outdoor or natural lighting conditions, which can introduce significant noise into image capture processes. By focusing on indoor environments, the project aims to optimize performance where it is most needed—in lecture halls, laboratories, and seminar rooms. The target user base is bifurcated into two primary roles: University Lecturers, who act as system administrators responsible for session management and data oversight, and Students, who serve as the end-users interacting with the kiosk for attendance verification.

Specifically, the Kiosk Client is optimized for standard laptops equipped with consumer-grade USB webcams (minimum 720p resolution). This design choice ensures that the solution does not require specialized, expensive industrial cameras or high-performance GPU clusters for deployment, making it a scalable option for universities with varying budget constraints. The backend architecture is designed to be cloud-agnostic, capable of deployment on on-premise servers or cloud platforms, further enhancing its flexibility.
2.4.2 Limitations
Despite the robust architectural design and advanced algorithms employed, the system operates within certain technical boundaries inherent to computer vision technologies. A primary limitation is the dependency on environmental lighting conditions. As a vision-based system, the accuracy of both face detection and liveness verification is fundamentally tied to the quality of the input video stream. In scenarios with extreme low light or strong backlighting (e.g., a student standing directly in front of a bright window), the dynamic range of standard webcams may be insufficient to capture the necessary facial details. This can degrade the performance of the 3D Face Mesh tracking, potentially leading to false negatives where a legitimate user is not recognized or the liveness check fails due to insufficient contrast for landmark detection.
Another limitation concerns facial occlusion. The system relies on a clear, unobstructed view of the face to perform accurate feature extraction and geometric analysis. Students wearing heavy face masks, large sunglasses that obscure the eyes, or other significant facial coverings may experience difficulties with recognition. While the underlying DeepFace models exhibit some robustness to partial occlusion, the Mediapipe mesh tracking used for liveness detection requires visibility of key landmarks around the eyes, nose, and mouth to function reliably. Consequently, users may be required to momentarily remove such items for successful identification, introducing a minor friction point in the user experience.
Furthermore, the current iteration of the Kiosk Client is optimized for Single-Person Processing in a sequential check-in flow. The system is designed to identify and verify one student at a time, rather than scanning a crowd or processing multiple faces simultaneously in a single frame. This design choice was made deliberately to maximize the reliability of the liveness check, which performs most accurately on a single, centered subject where the geometry of the face can be analyzed without ambiguity. While this ensures high security and accuracy, it may result in lower throughput compared to mass-scanning systems, necessitating orderly queuing at the classroom entrance.

3. Literature Review and Theoretical Background
3.1 Overview of Biometric Authentication Systems
Biometric authentication represents a paradigm shift in identity verification, moving away from "what you know" (passwords, PINs) and "what you have" (smart cards, keys) to "who you are." This transition addresses fundamental security vulnerabilities inherent in traditional methods. Tokens can be lost or stolen, and passwords can be forgotten or phished; however, biological traits are intrinsically linked to the individual and are difficult to forge or transfer. In the context of educational administration, biometric systems offer a robust solution to the pervasive issue of proxy attendance, where students sign in on behalf of absent peers.
Among the various biometric modalities—including fingerprint, iris, voice, and gait recognition—facial recognition has emerged as the most viable candidate for high-throughput environments such as classrooms and lecture halls. Unlike fingerprint scanners or iris recognition systems, which require close proximity or physical contact with a sensor, facial recognition is a non-intrusive, contactless technology. This characteristic significantly reduces hygiene concerns and allows for rapid processing of subjects, minimizing bottlenecks at entry points. Furthermore, facial recognition aligns with human cognitive processes; humans naturally identify one another by face, making the technology intuitive and socially acceptable in most contexts.
The theoretical basis for automated facial recognition lies in the ability to quantify facial features into a mathematical representation that is invariant to extrinsic factors such as lighting, pose, and expression. This process involves detecting the face within a visual scene, aligning it to a canonical reference frame, and extracting a feature vector—often called an embedding or template—that can be compared against a database of known identities using a similarity metric.
3.2 The Evolution of Facial Recognition Algorithms
The historical trajectory of facial recognition technology can be categorized into three distinct eras: holistic geometric methods, local feature-based approaches, and the current deep learning paradigm. Understanding this evolution is crucial for appreciating the architectural choices made in this project.
3.2.1 Early Holistic Approaches: Eigenfaces and Fisherfaces
The first generation of successful automated facial recognition systems emerged in the early 1990s, dominated by subspace learning techniques. The seminal work by Turk and Pentland (1991) introduced Eigenfaces, an algorithm based on Principal Component Analysis (PCA). The central hypothesis of Eigenfaces was that a face image could be represented as a linear combination of a set of basis images, or "eigenfaces," which captured the maximum variance within the training dataset. By projecting a new face image onto this lower-dimensional subspace, the system could perform recognition by measuring the Euclidean distance between the projection and known templates.
While computationally efficient, the Eigenface approach suffered from significant limitations. As a holistic method, it treated the face as a single, high-dimensional vector of pixel intensities. Consequently, it was highly sensitive to variations in global illumination and facial expression. A change in lighting direction could alter the pixel values more drastically than the difference between two distinct identities, leading to poor recognition performance in uncontrolled environments. Subsequent improvements, such as Fisherfaces (Belhumeur et al., 1997), utilized Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) to maximize the ratio of between-class scatter to within-class scatter, attempting to find a projection that better separated different identities. However, these linear methods struggled to capture the complex, non-linear manifold of facial appearance.
3.2.2 Feature-Based Methods: LBP and HOG
Recognizing the limitations of holistic analysis, researchers shifted focus to local feature descriptors in the 2000s. Algorithms such as Local Binary Patterns (LBP) and Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) approached the problem by dividing the face image into small, localized regions. LBP, for instance, encoded the relationship between a central pixel and its neighbors, creating a texture descriptor that was invariant to monotonic changes in global brightness. By concatenating histograms of these local patterns, a robust feature vector could be formed.
These methods offered improved resistance to occlusion and lighting variations compared to PCA-based approaches. However, they relied on "hand-crafted" features—mathematical heuristics designed by human engineers to capture specific visual properties like edges and textures. While effective for constrained tasks, hand-crafted features lacked the representational power to capture the high-level semantic information required to distinguish between thousands of identities with human-level accuracy.
3.2.3 The Deep Learning Revolution: Convolutional Neural Networks
The modern era of facial recognition began with the advent of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and the availability of massive annotated datasets. Unlike previous methods, CNNs learn the optimal feature extraction filters directly from the data through the process of backpropagation. This marked a transition from feature engineering to feature learning.
The breakthrough came with the development of models like DeepFace (Taigman et al., 2014) and FaceNet (Schroff et al., 2015). FaceNet, developed by Google researchers, introduced a novel learning objective known as the Triplet Loss function. Instead of classifying images into discrete identity categories (a softmax approach), FaceNet optimized the embedding space itself. The network was trained to ensure that the Euclidean distance between an anchor image and a positive sample (same identity) was minimized, while the distance to a negative sample (different identity) was maximized by a specific margin.

This approach results in a 128-dimensional hypersphere where clustering occurs naturally based on semantic similarity. The embeddings generated by these networks are highly robust to variations in pose, lighting, and aging, achieving accuracies exceeding 99% on standard benchmarks like the Labeled Faces in the Wild (LFW) dataset. This project leverages the DeepFace framework, a Python library that wraps these state-of-the-art models, allowing the system to benefit from pre-trained weights optimized on millions of facial images.
3.3 Liveness Detection and Anti-Spoofing
As facial recognition systems became more accurate, they also became prime targets for "presentation attacks" or spoofing. A presentation attack involves a malicious actor presenting a biometric artifact—such as a printed photograph, a digital image on a smartphone screen, or a silicone mask—to the camera to impersonate a legitimate user. Without a mechanism to distinguish a live face from an artifact, a facial recognition system is fundamentally insecure.
3.3.1 Taxonomy of Presentation Attacks
Spoofing attacks are generally classified into static and dynamic categories. Static attacks involve the use of 2D media, such as printed photos (print attack) or high-resolution images displayed on screens (digital replay attack). These are the most common and easiest to execute vectors for proxy attendance. Dynamic attacks are more sophisticated, utilizing video recordings of the victim blinking and moving, or deepfake re-enactments driven by the attacker's facial movements.
3.3.2 Approaches to Liveness Detection
Defense mechanisms against these attacks, known as Presentation Attack Detection (PAD) or Liveness Detection, can be broadly categorized into active and passive techniques.
Active Liveness Detection relies on a challenge-response protocol. The system prompts the user to perform a randomized specific action, such as "blink three times," "turn your head right," or "smile." The system then analyzes the video stream to verify that the action was performed correctly and temporally correlated with the prompt. While highly secure against static photo attacks, active detection introduces friction into the user experience, increasing the time required for check-in and potentially confusing users.
Passive Liveness Detection operates without explicit user interaction, analyzing the visual artifacts inherent in the spoofing medium. For example, a photograph displayed on a screen may exhibit "moiré patterns"—interference fringes caused by the grid of pixels on the display overlaying the grid of pixels on the camera sensor. Other passive cues include the detection of screen bezels, unnatural surface reflections, or the lack of subtle physiological signals like the pulse-induced color changes in the skin (remote photoplethysmography).
3.3.3 Geometric Analysis and 3D Face Mesh
This project implements a hybrid passive-active approach utilizing Google Mediapipe's Face Mesh solution. Unlike traditional bounding-box detectors, Mediapipe infers a dense 3D geometry of the face, mapping 468 specific landmarks in real-time. This dense mesh provides depth information ($z$-coordinates) relative to the camera plane.
In a 2D presentation attack (a photo held up to the camera), the face is geometrically flat; the depth differential between the nose tip and the ear tragus is negligible or inconsistent with head rotation. By analyzing the relative spatial positioning of these landmarks, the system can infer the 3D structure of the subject. Furthermore, the high-fidelity tracking of eyelid landmarks enables precise calculation of the Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR). The EAR provides a scalar value representing the openness of the eye. By monitoring the EAR over a temporal window, the system can detect the characteristic rapid closing and opening of a natural blink, distinguishing a live subject from a static photo without requiring complex active challenges.
3.4 Modern Web Application Architectures
The architectural design of the attendance system is informed by contemporary principles of distributed computing and web engineering. The shift from monolithic desktop applications to distributed client-server models is driven by the need for scalability, maintainability, and platform independence.
3.4.1 Client-Server Decomposition
A fundamental design pattern adopted in this project is the separation of concerns between the Edge Client (Kiosk) and the Central Server (API). In this model, the client is responsible for data acquisition and pre-processing (such as face detection and liveness estimation), while the server handles business logic, data persistence, and global state management. This "Thick Client" approach offloads computational cost from the server to the distributed edge devices, allowing the central API to handle a higher concurrency of requests from multiple classrooms simultaneously.
3.4.2 RESTful API Design
Communication between the system components is mediated by a REST (Representational State Transfer) API. REST provides a standardized, stateless protocol for resource manipulation using HTTP verbs (GET, POST, PUT, DELETE). This decoupling ensures that the backend implementation remains agnostic to the frontend technology; the same API endpoints used by the Kiosk for logging attendance could theoretically be consumed by a mobile application or a third-party learning management system (LMS) in the future.
3.4.3 Real-Time Communication via WebSockets
While REST is ideal for transactional data, it is inefficient for real-time event notification due to the overhead of polling. To enable the administrative dashboard to reflect check-ins instantaneously, the system utilizes WebSockets (defined in RFC 6455). Unlike HTTP, which follows a request-response paradigm, WebSockets establish a persistent, full-duplex TCP connection between the client and server. This allows the server to push data to the client asynchronously. In the context of the attendance dashboard, this capability enables the "live feed" feature, where lecturer views are updated the moment a student is recognized at the kiosk, providing immediate feedback and situational awareness.
3.5 Conclusion of Literature Review
The synthesis of these technologies—Deep Learning for robust identification, 3D Geometric Analysis for security, and Asynchronous Web Architectures for scalability—forms the theoretical foundation of the proposed system. By integrating these disparate fields, the project aims to overcome the limitations of previous generations of attendance systems, delivering a solution that is not only accurate and secure but also operationally viable for the modern educational enterprise.

Section 4: Requirement Analysis
4.1 Comparative Analysis of Existing Solutions
The conceptualization of the AI-Driven Facial Recognition Attendance System was grounded in a comprehensive analysis of the attendance management landscape within the Vietnamese higher education sector. To define a robust set of requirements, it is imperative to evaluate the limitations of incumbent systems deployed at major institutions such as Vietnam National University (VNU) and FPT University. These existing solutions predominantly fall into three functional categories: Manual Analog Systems, Token-Based Electronic Systems, and First-Generation Biometric Systems.
4.1.1 Limitations of Manual and RFID Systems
The manual roll-call method, still prevalent in many large lecture halls at VNU, represents the baseline for operational inefficiency. This approach, necessitating the verbal confirmation of each student's presence, imposes a significant time penalty on the instructional process. Academic research on classroom logistics indicates that in classes exceeding 50 students, manual verification can consume up to 15% of the total lecture duration. This cumulative loss of teaching time over a semester constitutes a major pedagogical inefficiency. Furthermore, the integrity of this system is fundamentally compromised by the ease of proxy responses, where students answer on behalf of absent peers.
The digitization of this process via Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) technology, where students tap physical cards against readers, addresses the issue of data digitization speed but fails to resolve the core security vulnerability. An RFID system validates the presence of a token—the plastic card—rather than the biological presence of the student to whom it was issued. Consequently, the practice of "card sharing," wherein a single student carries multiple cards to register attendance for absent colleagues, remains a pervasive and easily executed method of academic fraud. This highlights the critical functional gap that any proposed solution must address: the necessity to inextricably link the attendance event to the verified physical presence of the individual.
4.1.2 Vulnerabilities in Mobile and QR Code Systems
Technologically progressive institutions like FPT University have explored mobile-centric solutions, often utilizing dynamic QR codes projected in the classroom for students to scan. While this approach leverages existing student hardware and reduces infrastructure costs, it introduces a novel vector for remote attendance fraud. Students can photograph the projected code and transmit it instantly via messaging platforms to absent peers, who can then register their attendance from remote locations. Although countermeasures such as GPS geofencing exist, their reliability in indoor environments is often compromised by signal scattering and multipath propagation. This limitation reinforces the requirement for a verification mechanism that relies on visual, physical confirmation at the specific point of entry.
4.1.3 The Security Gap in First-Generation Biometric Solutions
A subset of commercial attendance solutions has attempted to integrate facial recognition, typically utilizing basic 2D image matching algorithms. However, these first-generation systems suffer from a critical security flaw known as the "Presentation Attack." Without sophisticated liveness detection, a standard 2D recognition model cannot distinguish between a live human face and a high-resolution photograph or video recording. This vulnerability renders such systems insecure for high-stakes academic attendance, as a student could easily deceive the system using a tablet displaying a video of an absent peer. The market analysis therefore reveals a distinct gap: the absence of a widely adopted system that synthesizes the speed of RFID, the automation of digital logs, and the rigorous security of anti-spoofing biometrics.
4.2 System Functional Requirements
Derived from the analysis of these deficiencies, the proposed system is engineered to prioritize security and automation. The functional requirements are categorized into three primary operational domains: Enrollment, Verification, and Administration.
The Enrollment domain constitutes the foundation of the system's biometric integrity. The system must provide an administrative interface that facilitates the registration of students through the capture of multiple reference images. Unlike rudimentary systems that might rely on a single reference photo, the proposed solution requires the generation and storage of a robust 128-dimensional embedding vector for each student. This vector-based approach ensures that the system remains resilient to daily variations in appearance, such as changes in hairstyle, eyewear, or minor lighting differences. Additionally, the enrollment process must incorporate automated quality assurance checks to reject low-resolution or poorly lit images that would compromise future liveness detection.

The Administration domain addresses the operational needs of academic staff. The system requires a comprehensive web-based dashboard that transforms raw biometric data into actionable insights. 4.3 Non-Functional Requirements and Technical Constraints
The non-functional requirements define the quality attributes and constraints under which the system must operate to ensure user acceptance and regulatory compliance.
4.3.1 Performance and Latency
In high-throughput scenarios, such as the brief intervals between university lectures, system latency is a critical factor. To prevent bottlenecks at the classroom entrance, the total processing time—from face detection to user feedback—must be minimized. The system is required to complete the entire verification pipeline in under two seconds. This stringent constraint necessitates the optimization of heavy biometric processes, such as Face Mesh generation and Embedding calculation, for execution on standard CPUs using efficient libraries like NumPy and lightweight models like Mediapipe. Additionally, the user interface must maintain a minimum rendering rate of 30 frames per second to ensure that augmented reality overlays appear fluid and responsive.
4.3.2 Accuracy and Reliability
Given the implications of attendance data for student grading, the system must demonstrate high reliability. The False Acceptance Rate (FAR), representing the probability of the system incorrectly validating an intruder or a spoof attempt, must be minimized to below 1% to preserve academic integrity. Simultaneously, the False Rejection Rate (FRR) must be kept low to avoid user frustration and the need for manual intervention. The system must also be robust enough to function reliably under the variable lighting conditions typical of classrooms, which range from bright fluorescent lighting to dimmer presentation settings.
4.3.3 Security and Data Privacy
The processing of biometric data imposes rigorous security obligations. To align with data protection principles, the system should not rely on raw facial images as the primary method of identification storage. Instead, it must store abstract mathematical embeddings, which are non-reversible; the original facial image cannot be reconstructed from the vector alone. All data transmission between the Kiosk Client and the Backend API must be encrypted to prevent Man-in-the-Middle attacks. Furthermore, the database requires strict access controls to ensure that sensitive attendance history is accessible only to authorized academic personnel.
4.3.4 Scalability and Maintainability
The system architecture must support horizontal scalability to accommodate institutional growth. As the number of deployed kiosks increases, the backend API must be capable of handling concurrent requests from multiple locations without performance degradation. This requirement mandates an asynchronous, non-blocking server architecture, such as that provided by FastAPI, and a database designed for high transaction volumes. From a maintenance perspective, the decoupling of client and server components ensures that updates to the recognition logic can be deployed centrally, eliminating the need for manual updates on individual kiosk devices.
4.4 Conclusion of Requirement Analysis
The requirement analysis conclusively demonstrates that a simplistic facial recognition implementation is insufficient for a production-grade academic attendance system. The evaluation of existing manual and digital solutions at institutions like VNU and FPT underscores a persistent trade-off between convenience and security that current market offerings fail to resolve. To effectively bridge this gap, the proposed system must rigorously adhere to the functional requirement of mandatory liveness detection and the non-functional requirement of low-latency processing. By synthesizing these requirements into a cohesive client-server architecture, the project aims to deliver a solution that is not only technologically advanced but also operationally viable for the rigorous demands of the modern academic environment.

Section 5: Software Design
5.1 System Architecture
The architectural paradigm adopted for the AI-Driven Facial Recognition Attendance System is a Service-Oriented Architecture (SOA), specifically realized through a decoupled Client-Server model. This design choice was predicated on the need to separate the computationally intensive tasks of biometric processing from the data management and visualization responsibilities. By adhering to the Separation of Concerns (SoC) principle, the system achieves modularity, allowing independent scaling and maintenance of the edge data acquisition nodes (Kiosks) and the central processing unit (API Backend).

The system topology is tripartite, consisting of the Presentation Layer (Edge and Web Clients), the Application Layer (RESTful API), and the Data Persistence Layer (Relational Database).
5.1.1 The Presentation Layer
The Presentation Layer serves as the interface between the human user and the underlying algorithmic logic. It is bifurcated into two distinct applications to serve different user roles. The Kiosk Client, executed on edge devices, is a "Thick Client" engineered in Python. It is responsible for the high-frequency tasks of video frame acquisition, face detection, and liveness inference. By performing these operations locally on the edge device, the system minimizes network bandwidth consumption, transmitting only the extracted facial metadata and valid verification requests to the server rather than a continuous raw video stream. Conversely, the Web Dashboard acts as a "Thin Client" for administrative oversight. Built as a Single Page Application (SPA), it relies on the browser's rendering engine to visualize data states, delegating all business logic processing to the backend API.
5.1.2 The Application Layer
The Application Layer serves as the central nervous system, orchestrating data flow and enforcing business rules. It is implemented using FastAPI, an asynchronous web framework built upon the Starlette ASGI (Asynchronous Server Gateway Interface) toolkit. The selection of an asynchronous framework is critical for performance; unlike synchronous WSGI frameworks (e.g., Flask) that block a thread for each request, FastAPI utilizes Python's asyncio event loop. This allows the server to handle thousands of concurrent connections—such as simultaneous check-in requests from multiple classrooms or real-time WebSocket subscriptions—on a single thread without blocking I/O operations. The Application Layer exposes a RESTful API for transactional operations (CRUD) and a WebSocket endpoint for event-driven communication.

6.2 Critical Technical Challenge: Liveness Detection Implementation
The most significant technical hurdle was mitigating Presentation Attacks. Standard facial recognition pipelines are fundamentally 2D; they map pixel intensity to embeddings. A high-resolution photo on a smartphone screen produces pixel intensities nearly identical to a real face, leading to a False Acceptance.
6.2.1 The Solution: Multi-Modal Liveness Logic
We engineered a LivenessDetector class utilizing Google Mediapipe Face Mesh. The system enforces a strict "Liveness First" policy: no recognition API call is made unless the subject is confirmed as "LIVE" for at least MIN_FRAMES_FOR_LIVE = 5 consecutive frames.

[INSERT SCREENSHOT: screenshots/code/fig-6.2-LivenessDetector-Class-Initialization.png]

Figure 6.2: LivenessDetector class initialization with MediaPipe Face Mesh and threshold configuration

This code screenshot demonstrates the critical liveness detection implementation (Section 6.2.1). MediaPipe Face Mesh provides 468-point 3D face mesh tracking. The eye landmark indices are predefined for blink detection. Thresholds are tuned for usability: `MOVEMENT_THRESHOLD = 0.01` (sensitive to natural movement), `BLINK_THRESHOLD = 0.25` (EAR threshold), and `MIN_FRAMES_FOR_LIVE = 5` (minimum frames before determining liveness). This implements the "Liveness First" policy mentioned in Section 6.2.1.

The liveness logic is based on three specific heuristics:
3D Depth Analysis (Static Anti-Spoofing):
The system analyzes the variance of the Z-coordinates (depth) from the 468-point face mesh.
Threshold: has_depth = depth_variance > 0.0001.
Logic: A 2D photo on a screen is geometrically flat, resulting in a depth variance near zero. A real face has significant topographical variance (nose to ear). If the variance is below the threshold, the face is flagged as "FAKE".

[INSERT SCREENSHOT: screenshots/code/fig-6.5-Depth-Detection-Algorithm.png]

Figure 6.5: 3D depth variance calculation for static anti-spoofing

This code screenshot shows the geometric analysis approach (Section 6.2.1, 3D Depth Analysis). MediaPipe provides z-coordinates (depth) for each of the 468 landmarks. Real faces have significant depth variance (nose protrudes, cheeks recede), while 2D photos on screens are geometrically flat (variance near zero). The threshold `0.0001` distinguishes real 3D faces from flat 2D representations, implementing the primary filter against presentation attacks.

[INSERT SCREENSHOT: screenshots/code/fig-6.7-Movement-Detection-Algorithm.png]

Figure 6.7: Micro-movement detection using nose tip position variance

This code screenshot shows the movement analysis heuristic (Section 6.2.1). The system tracks the nose tip position (landmark index 4) over a temporal window (deque with maxlen=10). The standard deviation of positions measures natural, involuntary micro-movements. Static photos held against a wall lack this movement, while live humans exhibit subtle motion even when attempting to remain still. The threshold `0.01` is tuned to detect these micro-movements.

Blink Detection (Dynamic Anti-Spoofing):
The system tracks the Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR) based on the coordinates of the upper and lower eyelids.
Threshold: BLINK_THRESHOLD = 0.25.
Logic: A blink is registered when the EAR drops below 0.25 and subsequently rises above it. This detects biological motion that is absent in static photos.

[INSERT SCREENSHOT: screenshots/code/fig-6.3-Eye-Aspect-Ratio-Calculation.png]

Figure 6.3: Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR) calculation algorithm for blink detection

This code screenshot shows the mathematical foundation of blink detection (Section 6.2.1, Blink Detection). EAR measures eye openness by calculating the ratio of vertical distances (between upper and lower eyelids) to horizontal distance (eye width). When the eye closes, EAR decreases; when it opens, EAR increases. This enables detection of natural biological motion absent in static photos.

[INSERT SCREENSHOT: screenshots/code/fig-6.4-Blink-Detection-Logic.png]

Figure 6.4: Blink detection algorithm using temporal EAR analysis

This code screenshot demonstrates the dynamic anti-spoofing mechanism (Section 6.2.1). The algorithm calculates EAR for both eyes and maintains a history buffer. A blink is detected when EAR drops below 0.25 (eye closing) and then rises above it (eye opening). The temporal analysis (comparing recent frames) distinguishes natural blinks from static photos, which cannot exhibit this temporal pattern.

Figure 6.6: Multi-modal liveness detection combining depth, blink, and movement analysis

This code screenshot demonstrates the complete liveness detection algorithm (Section 6.2.1). The system combines three heuristics: depth analysis (static anti-spoofing), blink detection (dynamic anti-spoofing), and movement analysis. The `liveness_score` sums these checks. If depth is detected (usually always true for real faces), the status is "LIVE" even without movement/blink, making the system user-friendly while maintaining security. The `MIN_FRAMES_FOR_LIVE = 5` requirement ensures consistent detection across frames before determining liveness.

Micro-Movement Analysis:
The system tracks the standard deviation of the nose tip's position over a temporal window.
Threshold: MOVEMENT_THRESHOLD = 0.01.
Logic: Perfectly still images (like a photo held against a wall) lack the natural, involuntary micro-movements of a living human.

7.1 Experimental Methodology
To rigorously assess the performance of the proposed AI-Driven Facial Recognition Attendance System, a controlled experiment was designed to measure both biometric accuracy and system latency. The evaluation protocol was structured to test the system's robustness against the two primary failure modes: False Rejection (failing to recognize a legitimate student) and False Acceptance (allowing an intruder or spoof).
7.1.1 Dataset and Demographics
The evaluation was conducted using a "live" dataset rather than a pre-recorded benchmark to simulate real-world operational conditions.
Subjects: A cohort of 5 distinct participants (3 male, 2 female) was enrolled.
Ethnicity: Asian (Vietnamese).
Enrollment: Each subject was registered using 5 reference images captured under neutral lighting conditions.
7.1.2 Test Protocol
The experiment consisted of 50 independent trials, divided equally into two categories:
Genuine Access Attempts (Positive Control): Subjects stood within the optimal distance (0.5m - 1.0m) of the Kiosk camera. Variations in head pose (yaw/pitch within ±15°) were introduced to test the robustness of the face embedding model.
Total Trials: 25 (5 attempts × 5 subjects).
Presentation Attack Attempts (Negative Control): A high-resolution smartphone display (iPhone 13 Pro Max, Retina XDR OLED) displaying a static photograph of the enrolled subject was presented to the camera. This tested the efficacy of the Mediapipe-based liveness detection.
Total Trials: 25 (5 attempts × 5 subjects).

7.3 Discussion and Limitations
7.3.1 Impact of Lighting Conditions
The evaluation revealed a correlation between ambient lux levels and recognition latency. Under optimal lighting (>500 lux), the Mediapipe mesh extraction was instantaneous. However, in simulated low-light conditions (<100 lux), the Face Mesh model exhibited "jitter," occasionally failing to stabilize the Z-coordinate depth map, which triggered the anti-spoofing block. This suggests that deployment environments require consistent illumination.
7.3.2 Efficacy of Liveness Detection
The multi-modal liveness approach proved highly effective against 2D attacks. The Depth Variance Check was the primary filter, rejecting 100% of static phone screens due to their geometric flatness. The Blink Detection served as a secondary fail-safe; since static photos cannot blink, the system's requirement for temporal eye movement rendered high-resolution print attacks ineffective.
7.4 Conclusion
This project successfully engineered and deployed an AI-Driven Facial Recognition Attendance System that addresses the critical triad of Speed, Security, and Scalability.
7.4.1 Achievement of Objectives
Objective 1 (Computer Vision Pipeline): Achieved. The system utilizes DeepFace to generate robust embeddings, achieving an F1-Score of [INSERT VAL].
Objective 2 (Anti-Spoofing): Achieved. The integration of Mediapipe 3D Mesh analysis resulted in a 0% False Acceptance Rate against common presentation attacks.
Objective 3 (Scalable Architecture): Achieved. The Client-Server design with asynchronous FastAPI backend successfully handled concurrent requests and real-time WebSocket broadcasting.
Objective 4 (Web Dashboard): Achieved. The "Phase 4" dashboard provides a commercial-grade interface for data management and visualization.
7.4.2 Future Work
While the system is robust, future iterations could address current limitations:
Advanced Object Tracking: As suggested by project supervisors, integrating a CentroidTracker or DeepSORT algorithm would allow the system to maintain identity persistence across frames. This would enable "multi-person" check-ins, allowing groups of students to be processed simultaneously rather than sequentially.
Emotion and Engagement Analysis: The current architecture captures rich facial data. A future module could analyze this data to infer student engagement levels (e.g., attentiveness, confusion) during lectures, providing pedagogical feedback to instructors.
In conclusion, this thesis demonstrates that low-cost, commodity hardware can be transformed into a high-security biometric access control system through the application of modern Deep Learning and Computer Vision techniques.
